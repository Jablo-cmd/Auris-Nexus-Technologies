# When should a business invest in custom software?

**Short answer:** invest in custom software when a process is important to how you operate or compete, when the tools you can buy force expensive workarounds, and when the cost of the current way of working is clearly higher than the cost of building and maintaining something better. If a product you can buy or configure already fits, use that instead.

## Seven signs it is time

1. **A critical process runs on spreadsheets.** Payroll inputs, leave, stock, client records or compliance tracking live in files that only one or two people understand.
2. **The same data is typed in more than once.** Staff re-capture information from email into a spreadsheet and then into another system.
3. **Managers cannot get a current view.** Reports are compiled by hand, are out of date by the time they arrive, and differ depending on who made them.
4. **Your process is a genuine differentiator.** The way you schedule, quote, deliver or serve clients is part of why customers choose you, and generic software would flatten it.
5. **You pay for several tools that don't talk to each other.** Licence costs and manual hand-offs add up.
6. **Growth multiplies the admin.** Every new branch, site or client adds a proportional amount of manual work.
7. **Compliance risk is rising.** Personal information sits in shared drives and inboxes with no access control or audit trail, which is a POPIA concern.

## When custom software is the wrong answer

- **The process is standard.** Accounting, payroll and email are well served by established products.
- **The process itself is unclear.** Software cannot fix a process that the business has not agreed on. Map it first.
- **There is no one to own it.** Custom software needs a business owner who makes decisions, tests, and drives adoption.

## A simple test

Estimate the hours a process takes each week, multiply by the people involved and their hourly cost, and annualise it. Compare that with the cost of building and maintaining a system over a few years. Include the less visible costs too: errors, delays, staff frustration and risk. If the gap is clear, it is worth a scoped conversation.

## How to start small

- Pick one process with a clear owner and a visible cost.
- Do a short discovery to define scope.
- Build the smallest version that removes the most manual work.
- Use it, measure it, then decide on the next phase.
