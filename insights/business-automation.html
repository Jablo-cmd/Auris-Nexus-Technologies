Automating the repetitive work that slows teams down

Business automation solutions are most valuable when they remove friction from work that is repetitive, predictable and high-volume. That gives teams more time for judgement, service and growth.

Most organisations do not need a large automation programme to see results. They need to find the few processes where people spend hours moving information from one place to another, and fix those properly. This article explains how to find them, how to automate them well, and what to avoid.

Find the friction

Look for tasks that involve repeated data entry, approvals, notifications or report generation. These are usually the fastest places to create a meaningful return.

A useful way to spot them is to follow a single piece of work from start to finish: a leave request, a new client, a purchase order or a delivery. Note every point where someone re-types information, forwards an email, chases an approval or copies figures into a spreadsheet. Each of those points is a candidate for automation.

Common examples include:

  • Leave, expense and purchase requests that travel by email and wait in inboxes.
  • New client or employee details captured on a form and then typed into several systems.
  • Weekly or monthly reports assembled by hand from exports and spreadsheets.
  • Reminders for renewals, expiries and follow-ups that depend on someone remembering.
  • Status updates that customers or managers have to phone in to get.

Decide what to automate first

Not every candidate deserves to be automated, and not all at once. A simple scoring approach helps you pick the first one:

  • Volume: how often the task happens each week or month.
  • Time: how long it takes each time, including waiting and chasing.
  • Errors: how often mistakes happen and what they cost to fix.
  • Stability: whether the process is settled, or still changing every few weeks.

The best first candidate is frequent, time-consuming, error-prone and stable. A process that is still being redesigned should be settled before it is automated, otherwise you automate the confusion.

Automate the flow, not just the task

The biggest gains come from connecting steps end to end. A form that updates the right system, triggers the next action and notifies the right person can save hours of manual coordination.

Take a leave request as an example. Automating only the form saves little if the request still arrives as an email that a manager must remember to answer. Automating the flow means the request goes to the right manager, reminds them if it waits too long, updates the leave balance when it is approved, tells the employee the outcome and shows HR the current picture, all without anyone copying information between systems.

Keep people in the loop where it matters

Automation should handle the routine path and hand the unusual cases to a person. Decide up front which situations need human judgement, such as a request outside policy, a complaint or a payment above a threshold, and route those to the right person with all the context they need. Customers and staff notice when a system makes them feel unheard; good automation makes service faster without making it colder.

Measure the result

Before you automate, record how the process performs today: how long it takes from start to finish, how many people touch it, and how often it goes wrong. Measure the same things a month after go-live. Those numbers show whether the work paid off and help you decide what to automate next.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Automating a broken process instead of fixing it first.
  • Building automations that only one person understands, with no documentation or owner.
  • Connecting systems without agreeing which one holds the correct version of the data.
  • Ignoring exceptions, so unusual cases fall through the cracks.
  • Skipping training, so people keep using the old workaround alongside the new flow.

Choose the right tool for the job

There are three broad ways to automate a process, and the right one depends on its complexity:

  • Use what you already own. Microsoft 365, Google Workspace and many accounting or CRM products include forms, approval flows and alerts that cover simple processes with no new licence.
  • Use an automation platform. Integration tools can move information between cloud products you already use, which suits straightforward hand-offs between two or three systems.
  • Build it into a business system. When a process has several roles, approval rules, exceptions and reporting needs, it is usually more reliable as part of a purpose-built system than as a chain of separate automations.

Whichever route you choose, make sure someone owns the automation, it is documented, and the team knows what to do when it does not behave as expected.

Work out what it is worth

As an illustration only: if a team processes 120 requests a month and each one takes 20 minutes of typing, forwarding and chasing, that is 40 hours a month. If automation removes three quarters of that effort, it frees about 30 hours a month, about three quarters of a 40-hour working week, before counting fewer errors and faster responses. Doing this sum for your own processes makes it much easier to decide where automation is worth the investment.

Key takeaways

  • Focus on processes that are repeatable and visible across the business.
  • Design automation around service quality, not speed alone.
  • Keep humans involved where empathy, judgment or exception handling matters.
  • Measure before and after, so you know what the automation is worth.

Implementation considerations

Good automation requires clear process mapping, thoughtful user adoption and reliable integration with the systems already in use. That is what makes the result sustainable.

Speed and quality stop being a trade-off when the routine work runs itself.
